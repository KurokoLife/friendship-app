# Core: what Okage believes

Written 2026-11-15 with the founder. This is the one place Okage's beliefs are written. Every other file points here instead of repeating them. **Every feature, screen and line of copy is tested against the full version below, not the short one.**

Name: Okage (おかげ) is chosen. Until the trademark, domain, app store and pronunciation checks pass (see 02 Plan and status), the app, store listings and code still say "Limen".

Other files: 01 Product (what's built today and how it looks), 02 Plan and status (decided but not built, open decisions, to-dos), 03 How we work (repo, database, testing). The brand story (for outside the app) lives only in the founder's project.

---

## Short version

**Become aware of what you can't always see: no one gets where they are alone, and you make a difference to others. Then act on what you already value: treat people the way you'd want to be treated, even when you make a mistake, and ask when you don't know.**

---

## Full version

### 1. What Okage means

- In everyday Japanese, おかげ (お陰, 御蔭) acknowledges the help or influence behind a good outcome; おかげさまで means roughly "thanks to you (and others), things are well". 陰 means shade: support that sits behind the scenes.
- Our reading: some of what sustains our lives is what we don't see or touch. Above all, the people who think of us, spend time with us and would lend a hand.
- It works both ways. Other people's quiet presence holds us up, and our own small acts hold others up, often without us ever seeing the effect. Each of us can be someone's okage without knowing it.
- If we don't pay attention to what we can't see or touch, we miss what's great about life.
- It is **not** a religious idea, **not** about owing people because they helped us, **not** obligation, **not** an exchange, and not simply "be grateful". It is our interpretation inspired by the word, not a claim about its dictionary meaning.

### 2. How awareness becomes action

- Awareness alone rarely changes what people do. Knowing something and acting on it are different steps.
- What turns awareness into action is **the values a person already holds**. "I value honesty, so I'm honest."
- These are values people have now, not a person they're trying to become. "Who I want to be" points to a gap; Okage starts from what's already there.
- Acting from your own values lasts. Acting from guilt, pressure, rewards or for a reaction fades.
- So the chain is: **awareness → the values you already hold → action.** Okage helps with the first step and makes the last step easy. The middle step always belongs to the person.

### 3. The golden rule

**Treat people the way you'd want to be treated.**
- Put yourself in their place and act the way you'd want to be treated. You show up, tell people early, answer every hi and end things honestly because that's how you'd want it done to you.
- The reason comes from you, so the act is complete once you've done it, whatever the other person does.
- **What it is not:** "do it because that's what they want or like" or "do it so they'll like you". That puts pressure on the other person, and when they don't react the way you hoped, you feel let down. Effort done for a reaction turns friendship into a trade.

**Showing you care is part of it** (founder, Nov 15).
- Caring about people is one of the values people already hold. A check-in, a call, asking how something went, showing up: these are ways a person shows they care. That's about what **you** do, so it's in line with the golden rule.
- Everyone shows they care in their own way, and so do the people around them.
- The line to hold: **naming what the act is from your side is fine; promising what it does to the other person is not.**
  - Fine: "A check-in is one way to show someone you're thinking of them." (what your act is)
  - Not fine: "They'll love hearing from you", "It means more to them than people expect", "Most people are glad to be asked" (a claim about their reaction)
- Don't treat every line that mentions caring as a reaction claim. Ask: is this about the person's own act, or about how the other person will feel?

**Even when you make a mistake.**
- Everyone falls short sometimes: a flaked plan, a slow reply, a careless word. That's being human.
- If someone mistreated you, you'd want to hear "I'm sorry" rather than be ignored. So when you get it wrong, you own it and say sorry.
- Whether to accept is the other person's choice, because that's the choice you'd want for yourself.
- You apologise because it's how you'd want to be treated, not to be forgiven. The apology is complete once it's said.
- A mistake is something you did, never who you are.

### 4. Ask when you don't know

- Imagining someone's place tells you how to treat them. It doesn't tell you what they think, mean, feel or enjoy. For that, ask.
- Asking is the golden rule too: you'd want to be asked, not guessed about.
- "Not sure what they meant? Ask them. Only they know."

### 5. Honest and respectful together

- People can say what they want, see the other side, and part kindly if it doesn't fit.
- Choosing to say something and choosing to keep it to yourself are both right, depending on what the person wants. Okage helps people notice, if they want to. It never pushes either way.
- Background (internal only, never in the app or marketing): in more group-minded cultures people often care a lot but avoid honesty to keep the peace; in more individual-minded cultures people are more direct, but flaking and careless replies are common. The golden rule answers both: you're honest because you'd want honesty, and you show up because you'd want someone to show up.

### 6. What we assume about people

- **Most people hold good values, because most do.** Kindness, honesty, helpfulness and responsibility rank as the most important values across cultures, and people act more honestly than others expect.
- **Many people act on them when they see others do.** About half of people cooperate when they see others cooperate. So the norms Okage sets and makes visible (answer every hi, show up, end honestly) carry real weight.
- **Values come in different orders.** Some people put honesty first, others keeping the peace. Okage never judges which order is right.
- **The golden rule doesn't depend on everyone valuing the same things.** It only needs people to know how they'd want to be treated, and nearly everyone wants honesty and wants people to show up for them.
- **A few don't share good values.** Okage is built so they can't harm anyone: selfie check, Report, Block, the scam note, chat limits, and nothing private ever shown.
- **Freedom to choose includes freedom to get it wrong.** Okage doesn't police whether people act well. It only protects others from harm.

### 7. What the app does, and never does

The app's job is small:
- **Helps people notice:** a person, a moment, the difference they make.
- **Makes the step easy** when the moment comes: one suggestion at a time, a starter they finish themselves, an easy way to plan.

The app never:
- Supplies the reason to act. It doesn't tell people what they should value or who they are, and it never labels or praises them ("You're a great friend!").
- Writes, rewrites or polishes a message to another person. Starters are fine; the person finishes them in their own words. An apology is never a starter: it has to come from the person.
- Reads message content. Reminders use timing only. The one exception is the scam word check, which runs on the receiver's own phone and stores nothing.
- Acts for someone, decides for them, or interprets the other person for them.
- Scores, ranks or counts people, or reads activity as feeling. A quiet stretch doesn't mean someone cares less.
- Shames anyone, keeps a visible record of their mistakes, or uses guilt, pressure or rewards.
- Pushes anyone to accept an apology, or tells the sender whether it was accepted.
- Shows anyone another person's private answers.
- Sells more matches, shows ads, or sells anyone's information.

### 8. What friendship is

- **Made of small, ordinary moments, in person.** A quick call, a coffee, a walk, stopping by, repeated over time. Okage is for meeting in person; chatting is how people get there.
- **Effort is the care.** A message someone wrote themselves matters because they chose to make the effort. Small acts like a check-in are ways a person shows they care (see section 3).
- **Some friction is how it grows.** Okage eases the hard parts but doesn't remove them. The hard parts are where friendship is built.
- **The hard parts Okage eases:** too many options and too little focus; fear of rejection and second-guessing; staying in your own head instead of asking; effort that doesn't come back; never actually meeting; fading after the first meetups; meeting a stranger safely; endings without honesty.
- **Success is people you could name:** friends you'd call with good news, meet for no reason, or ask for a hand, and who could ask you too.
- **Okage succeeds when the friendships continue without it.** Graduation is a step, not the goal. What people learn carries over to every friendship they have.
- Okage is for friendship only: not dating, romance or networking. Romantic or sexual advances, or asking for money, lead to removal.

### 9. Every profile is a person

Behind each profile is someone with their own life, hopes and nerves.
- Discover leads with the person's own words and face, never a grid or a stack to flip through.
- No scores or rankings of people: no "92% match", no counts of likes, views or matches, anywhere.
- Decisions sound human: "Not now", never "Pass", "Nope" or "Skip". No swipe gestures.
- People are always called by name, never "a match", "a user" or "a profile" in app copy.
- A hi starts from them: starters come from something they wrote.
- Endings stay kind and clear, never silence.
- The founder's inspiration for this is a Japanese idea; it is never named or explained in the app.

## The test for every feature

Every feature, screen and line of copy must pass all seven. If it fails one, it changes or it goes.

1. **It helps people notice, not obey.** It brings something into view (a person, a moment, the difference they make) and leaves the decision to them.
2. **The reason comes from their own values.** Anything that moves someone to act draws on what they already value: how they'd want to be treated, caring about the people in their life. Never guilt, pressure, rewards or a promise about the other person's reaction. Naming an act as a way to show you care passes (section 3). Check: someone who hasn't reached out to anyone in months could read it without feeling judged.
3. **It asks instead of guessing.** Wherever someone else's thoughts, meaning or wishes come up, it points to asking them, never to guessing or explaining them on their behalf.
4. **The effort and the choice stay theirs.** Their words, their decision, their act. The app can make the step easier but never takes it. Saying no or turning it off is always an equal option.
5. **The other person stays a person.** It's aimed at a real relationship, ideally time together in person, and never turns anyone into a score, count, ranking or "a profile".
6. **It's safe even for the few who don't share good values.** Nobody can use it to pressure, deceive or harm someone, and private stays private.
7. **It makes Okage less needed over time.** What the person learns or does carries over to friendships outside the app.

Also: if a feature adds time in the app without helping a friendship, it doesn't belong.

Passing point 2 does not mean adding a line. Most screens pass by simply not giving a bad reason: no line at all is often the right answer.

## How it should feel (read this before checking any copy)

The test is not a word check. The beliefs are **felt in how something reads**, not spelled out on screen. A line passes when it brings something into view and then gets out of the way. It fails when it explains, lectures, pushes, makes the person dwell, or sounds like an app talking about itself.

Ask of every line:
- Does it show, or does it explain? (Showing: "Most friendships don't end. They drift." Explaining: stating "no one gets where they are alone" on a screen.)
- Is the person at the center, or the app?
- Would a thoughtful friend say this, or does it sound like an app, a coach or a therapist?
- Does "you" risk stinging someone whose friendships have thinned? "Us" and "we" often read kinder.
- Is it needed at all? Fewer words is usually better.
- Am I applying a rule literally where the founder would hear it differently?

Lines the founder approved, and why (Nov 15):
- "Most friendships don't end. They drift." Brings an unseen thing into view; no judgement.
- "Friendships are made of small moments. A quick call. A coffee. Stopping by to say hi. It's easy to overlook the small things that keep us close. We each show we care in our own way, and so do the people around us. They hold us up when we need them, and we try to do the same for them." (intro, the founder's own wording) Shows the quiet, two-way support behind friendship without naming it; "us", not "you".
- "Okage comes from a Japanese word for the quiet help behind the good things in life, the kind that often goes unnoticed." (About) Plain; "unnoticed", because the help is there to notice.
- "Not sure what they meant? Ask them. Only they know." Ask, in one line.
- "Plans fall through for all kinds of reasons. If you'd like to know what happened, you can ask." Eases worry, points to asking, leaves the choice.
- "A check-in is one way to show someone you're thinking of them." About your own act, not their reaction.
- "No thanks is fine. A clear answer is kinder than no answer." Kept as is; not every line needs the golden rule.

Lines the founder rejected, and why (Nov 15):
- "Is hurting someone's feeling something you want to do?" Questions the person's character and implies harm. Never.
- "Sarah may be wondering what she said wrong." Guesses the other person's feelings.
- "They told you instead of going quiet. That's the kind way to end things." Makes people dwell and tells them how to feel.
- A "would you want..." golden rule question on every card. Repetitive and annoying; it loses its meaning.
- An "I'm sorry, I need to..." starter. An apology has to come from the person.
- "It usually means more to them than people expect." / "Most people are glad to be asked..." Promise the other person's reaction.
- "Limen helps you make more of them." Puts the app at the center.
- Spelling out the Core on a screen (for example "no one gets where they are alone"). Literal; the app shows it, it doesn't say it.

A mistake to avoid (Nov 15): "A check-in tells someone they matter to you" was flagged as a reaction claim by applying the rule literally. The founder's point: a check-in is a way to show you care, which is the person's own act. Judge the spirit, not the words.

Add to these lists whenever the founder approves or rejects a line.

## Voice and copy rules

Okage has one voice, inside the app and outside it.

**Everywhere (app, website, email, pitch):**
- A steady, thoughtful friend to anyone of any gender. Calm, warm, honest, grown-up. Quietly encouraging, never a coach or a therapist.
- Plain, short and direct. Lead with doing (catch up, get together, tell them, see how it's going, note it), not feeling.
- Small moments and activities count as friendship.
- Notice, don't instruct. One true line, not a lecture.
- No guilt, pressure, urgency or exaggerated promises. Never make someone with no friends feel bad.
- Never promise the other person's reaction as the reason to act: no "they'll love it", "it lands better than you think", "shows you were listening", "it means more to them than people expect", "most people are glad to be asked". Naming what the act is from the person's own side is fine; see section 3.
- For what someone means, feels or enjoys, point to asking ("Ask them. Only they know."), never to guessing ("How's that going, do you think?").
- Calm, true lines that ease a person's own worry are fine (the liking gap, "most people feel a bit nervous"), as long as they don't promise how the other person will react.
- No therapy or self-help language, no dating words, no gamification, no inspirational clichés, nothing that makes friendship sound like a productivity task.
- Words to avoid: share, open up, connection, on your mind, care, journey, vulnerable, reflect, practice, skill, habit, get better at. ("Care" is fine in "show you care", about the person's own act.)
- Test: would a 50-year-old man and a 35-year-old woman both read it without wincing?

**App only:**
- No em dashes.
- Never "AI", never "your turn".
- **Don't overdo the golden rule** (founder, Nov 15). Shown everywhere, it loses its meaning and gets annoying. A golden rule question ("If you were the one waiting, how would you want to hear?") appears only at a handful of moments where it truly fits, never as the reason line on every card. Optional, no answer needed, never guilt. The current list of places is in 02 Plan and status (copy update).
- Don't add lines that make people dwell on a moment or tell them how to feel about it. Fewer words is usually better.
- The beliefs are shown, not explained. They're stated once, on the About page; everywhere else the app acts on them without naming them.
- Never ask people what kind of friend they are or what they value.
- Never in the app: "Have you been focusing on the wrong things?" or anything suggesting the person has neglected people (that belongs to the founder's story), anything that makes friendship sound like an exchange or an obligation, or religious readings of the name.

The website and founder's story can be a little longer and more personal than app copy. They still sound like the same steady friend.

## The research behind it

- **Awareness isn't enough.** Health knowledge rarely produces lasting behaviour change on its own; calorie labels raise awareness without reducing what people eat. A medium-to-large change in intention leads to only a small-to-medium change in behaviour (Webb and Sheeran 2006, 47 experiments).
- **Your own values make it last.** Behaviour done from values a person personally endorses lasts; behaviour done from guilt, obligation or for approval doesn't (self-determination theory, Deci and Ryan). Help done from your own values benefits both people, and recipients feel more grateful for it (Weinstein and Ryan 2010).
- **Friends don't keep score.** In close relationships people give without expecting repayment; expecting it and not getting it makes people feel used (Clark and Mills). Putting others' wishes first for approval is linked to distress and holding back what you need (Helgeson and Fritz).
- **Asking beats imagining.** Imagining someone's view doesn't make you more accurate about what they think; asking does (Eyal, Steffel and Epley 2018, 25 experiments). Imagining yourself in someone's place can raise your own worry, so golden rule prompts stay light (Batson 1997).
- **Honesty goes better than expected.** Honest conversations go better than people predict (Levine and Cohen 2018). People overestimate how upset others are when they decline (Givi and Kirk 2023); what upsets people is a lie about the reason. People underestimate how often others say yes to a direct ask (Flynn and Bohns 2008).
- **Mistakes and repair.** Feeling bad about what you did (guilt) leads to apology and making amends; feeling bad about who you are (shame) leads to hiding and blaming others (Tangney). Being kind to yourself about a mistake makes people more motivated to make amends, not less (Breines and Chen 2012).
- **Most people hold good values.** Benevolence (helpful, honest, forgiving, responsible) ranks as the most important value across cultures (Schwartz and Bardi 2001). In 40 countries, people returned lost wallets more often when they held more money, and even experts expected the opposite (Cohn et al. 2019).
- **Many cooperate when others do.** About half of people cooperate when they see others cooperate; about a third don't (Fischbacher, Gächter and Fehr 2001). Roughly 7% of people show mostly "dark" traits, about half mostly "light" ones (Kaufman).
- **In more group-minded cultures** people ask for support less, out of concern for the relationship (Kim, Sherman and Taylor 2008), which is why keeping something to yourself stays a real, equal choice.
- **Friendship takes time together:** roughly 94 hours to become casual friends and 164 to become friends (Hall). Text carries less warmth than voice or meeting in person. More options lead to more rejecting (Pronk and Denissen).
